Section 1: Introduction

Sharks and Minnows is a group tag game. Minnows try to cross an open playing area while sharks try to tag them. As minnows are caught, they become sharks, making each crossing a little trickier. The last minnow remaining wins.

This recreational rulebook describes a land-based version with growing numbers of sharks. The game has no single governing body, official field dimensions, or universally binding rulebook. The procedures below establish a complete house ruleset; groups should agree on any variations before starting.

  • Crossing: One trip from the starting safe zone to the opposite safe zone.
  • Round: A series of crossings ending when one or no minnows remain.
  • Shark: A player who tags minnows in the active playing area.
  • Minnow: A player who has not yet been caught.

The game rewards timing, changes of direction, and awareness of other players. Sharks catch with a gentle touch, never by holding, tackling, or physically blocking someone.

Section 2: Equipment

No equipment or special uniform is required for this land-based ruleset. Players need an appropriate open space with clearly understood boundaries. Existing surface lines or agreed landmarks can identify the playing area and safe zones without additional objects.

  • Optional boundary markers: Soft markers can help show sidelines and end lines. Position them outside running paths so they do not create obstacles.
  • Optional role identifiers: Bibs can distinguish sharks from minnows, but verbal identification works too.
  • Optional organizer tools: A whistle or timer can help manage a large group. Spoken signals are sufficient.
  • Recommended clothing: Wear clothing that permits easy movement and footwear suited to the surface. Secure loose laces and remove dangling accessories.

There is no ball, goal, or other object to carry, defend, or collect. Optional equipment does not change what counts as a successful crossing or a legal tag.

Section 3: Playing Area

Choose a flat, clear area with two opposite end lines and two sidelines. The space between the end lines is the active tagging area, sometimes called the ocean. Beyond each end line is a safe zone where minnows gather between crossings.

There are no official measurements. As a suggested setup for a small group, use an active rectangle approximately 15 metres long and 10 metres wide, with at least 3 metres of clear stopping space beyond each end line. These are adjustable starting dimensions, not standardized requirements; provide more stopping room whenever speed or conditions demand it.

  • Make the area wide enough for players to pass without squeezing through tight gaps.
  • Keep safe zones large enough for all remaining minnows to gather without crowding.
  • Place boundaries well away from roads, steps, trees, equipment, walls, and other hazards.
  • Explain exactly which lines or landmarks define the boundaries before play begins.

For this ruleset, a minnow reaches safety when both feet are completely beyond the destination end line. A player with a foot on that line is still taggable. Sharks must stay inside the active area and cannot reach into a safe zone to tag a player who has arrived.

The sidelines remain part of the playing area. Placing a foot on the ground completely outside a sideline counts as going out of bounds. Adapt the crossing and boundary criteria before play for participants using mobility devices.

Section 4: Players & Officials

Begin with one shark and at least two minnows. Larger groups can start with two or more sharks if everyone agrees. There is no fixed team size; the usable space and players' ability to move safely determine a sensible group size.

Choose the first shark by volunteering, a random selection, or a rotation. The shark starts near the middle of the active area. All minnows begin together in one safe zone, facing the opposite end.

  • Minnows attempt each crossing, acknowledge valid tags, and wait in the destination safe zone after arriving.
  • Sharks chase and gently tag minnows while respecting boundaries and giving other players room.
  • A caller announces starts, ends crossings, and pauses play when necessary. A participating player can fill this role.
  • An organizer is useful for children's groups to explain rules, settle disputes, and monitor spacing. Formal referees are unnecessary.

Players arriving late should join as minnows at the beginning of the next round. Anyone may leave the game or request a pause. If the group becomes crowded, divide it into smaller games rather than packing more players into the same space.

Section 5: Rules of Play

  1. Set up: Identify the boundaries, select the starting shark or sharks, and gather minnows in one safe zone.
  2. Start the crossing: The caller says, “Sharks and minnows, go!” Sharks may move anywhere inside the active area after this signal.
  3. Leave the safe zone: Minnows choose their moment to enter. To prevent indefinite waiting, this ruleset uses a spoken five-count after the start signal. Every minnow must enter by the end of that count.
  4. Cross the ocean: Minnows run or use the agreed movement style toward the opposite safe zone. They may dodge, slow down, and change direction within the boundaries, but must continue attempting the crossing.
  5. Make a legal tag: A shark gently touches a minnow's upper arm, shoulder, or upper back with an open hand. Tags must occur before the minnow has reached safety. Touching loose clothing alone does not count.
  6. Handle a catch: A tagged minnow acknowledges the tag and moves carefully to the nearest sideline waiting area, outside the active space. They become a shark for the next crossing and cannot tag anyone during the current crossing.
  7. Finish the crossing: Continue until every minnow has reached the destination safe zone or been caught. Safe minnows stay there and do not re-enter the active area.
  8. Reset: New sharks join the existing sharks in the active area. Surviving minnows cross back in the opposite direction on the next start signal.
  9. End the round: Apply the winning conditions in the scoring section, then select a starting shark for another round.

After entering the ocean, a minnow cannot use the starting safe zone as a refuge. Returning fully into it counts as being caught under this ruleset. A player who misses the five-count also joins the sharks on the next crossing, unless an accessibility arrangement was agreed beforehand.

Common house rules and variations

  • Immediate conversion: Caught minnows become active sharks immediately. Announce this change clearly; it makes crossings harder and increases nearby changes of direction.
  • Stationary seaweed: Caught minnows stay in place and tag within arm's reach without stepping, grabbing, or leaning into another player's path. Use generous spacing and stop if stationary players become obstacles.
  • Walking version: Everyone walks. This can suit smaller spaces or groups with a wider range of mobility and confidence.
  • Multiple starting sharks: Begin with extra sharks for a large group or a shorter round.
  • Different start calls: Groups may use a chant or call-and-response, provided everyone knows the exact signal that starts play.

Swimming-pool and ball-dribbling versions also share the name, but require separate equipment, playing conditions, and safety procedures. They are outside this land-based ruleset.

Section 6: Scoring

The standard game uses survival rather than points. Each successful crossing keeps a minnow in the round, while a catch changes that player's role to shark. A caught player stays involved rather than being eliminated from the whole game.

  • One minnow remains: Stop play and declare that minnow the winner. They do not have to complete an extra crossing alone.
  • Several minnows survive a crossing: Start another crossing in the opposite direction.
  • The final minnows are caught together: If no sole survivor can be identified, those last minnows share the win. Do not invent an order for an indistinguishable finish.
  • A round ends early: If play must stop while multiple minnows remain, record them as survivors without declaring a sole winner.

For the next round, this ruleset makes the previous winner the starting shark. After a shared win, choose one of the winners randomly. Groups may instead rotate the starting role or choose the first minnow caught, but should agree before the first round.

As an optional scoring variation, give each minnow one point for every completed crossing and compare totals over an agreed number of rounds. Rotate starting sharks so everyone receives comparable chances to score. This is a house scoring system, not an official ranking method.

Section 7: Violations & Penalties

Use simple, consistent consequences that keep the game moving. Ordinary boundary or start errors change a player's role; unsafe contact requires an immediate pause and correction.

  • Minnow goes out of bounds: Treat the minnow as caught. They wait outside the active area and join the sharks on the next crossing.
  • Minnow returns to the starting safe zone: Treat the minnow as caught once they return fully across the starting end line.
  • Minnow does not enter by the five-count: Treat the minnow as caught, except where a different start arrangement was agreed.
  • False start: Stop and reset the crossing. Explain the signal again before restarting.
  • Shark crosses a boundary: Any tag made while outside the active area is invalid. The shark must return inside before tagging again.
  • Tag after safety: The tag does not count. The minnow remains a minnow.
  • Premature tagging by a caught minnow: The tag is invalid under the default next-crossing conversion rule.
  • Grabbing, pushing, tripping, tackling, or deliberate body blocking: Stop play. The contact is not a valid tag. Explain the safe technique; repeated or serious conduct means sitting out the round.

If unsafe play or interference prevents a fair crossing, let the affected minnow reach the destination safe zone without being chased, or replay the crossing if several players were affected. A player forced outside a boundary by prohibited contact should not receive the normal out-of-bounds consequence.

For a disputed tag, pause briefly and hear what the players observed. If the caller cannot resolve whether a legal tag happened before safety, keep the player as a minnow. Respect the decision and continue; honest uncertainty should not turn into an argument.

Section 8: Safety Considerations

Inspect the surface before play and remove loose obstacles. Avoid slippery ground, holes, sharp debris, and areas where players could run into traffic or hard structures. Leave room beyond the end lines so reaching safety does not require an abrupt stop.

  • Touch gently: Use a light, open-hand tag on the agreed areas. Never target the head, neck, chest, or groin.
  • Stay upright: No diving, sliding tackles, wrestling, or jumping into other players.
  • Keep paths open: Sharks may position themselves to attempt tags, but cannot link arms, form a physical barrier, or trap a player against a boundary.
  • Watch the space: Look where you are going and slow down near crowds, safe zones, and players leaving after a tag.
  • Stop for a fall: Anyone may call “Stop!” Players stop moving, give the fallen person space, and wait for the caller to restart play.
  • Adjust intensity: Use walking, fewer players, or shorter rounds when the group needs a calmer pace.
  • Take breaks: Allow rest and water breaks, and stop when conditions or fatigue make running unsafe.

Agree on inclusive movement and tagging arrangements before the round. For participants using mobility devices, define when the player has crossed an end line and choose a reachable body tagging area with their agreement. Never grab, push, or tag moving wheels or other device components.

This file covers play on land. Do not transfer its chasing and tagging procedures directly into water. A swimming version needs separate water-specific rules, appropriate supervision, and compliance with the pool's requirements; underwater restraint, dunking, and breath-holding contests must not be part of play.
