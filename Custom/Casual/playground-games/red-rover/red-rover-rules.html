Section 1: Introduction

Red Rover is a traditional playground team game in which two opposing lines take turns calling a runner from the other team. In the classic version, the runner attempts to pass through a chosen link in the opposing line. A successful runner takes a player back to the runner's team; an unsuccessful runner joins the defending team.

There is no universal governing body or single official rulebook for Red Rover. Customs vary by school, camp, neighborhood, and recreation program, so the group should agree on its version before play begins. These rules present a clear recreational format while identifying common variations and a safer non-impact alternative.

Object of the Game

The usual objective is to bring every player onto one team. Teams accomplish this by winning challenges during alternating turns. For a shorter game, players may instead complete a fixed number of turns and compare team sizes or points.

Spirit of Play

Red Rover should be cooperative, controlled, and welcoming. Players must not use excessive force, target someone because of size or ability, or ridicule a player who declines to run. The supervising adult or game leader may modify or stop play whenever conditions become unsafe.

Section 2: Equipment

No equipment is required. Red Rover is played using only the participants and a suitable open area.

Optional Setup Aids

A group may use removable boundary indicators to show the two line positions or the outer limits of the playing area, but these aids are optional and are not necessary to compete. If used, they should be flat, soft, highly visible, and placed where players will not trip over them.

Clothing and Personal Items

Comfortable clothing and secure, activity-appropriate footwear are recommended but are not prescribed as game equipment. Players should remove or secure jewelry, hard accessories, loose cords, and objects carried in pockets. Clothing must not be grabbed during play.

Pre-Game Inspection

The game leader should inspect the area and ensure that no optional setup aid creates a hard edge, entanglement risk, or obstacle. Because the game requires no equipment, adding padding or other objects does not make uncontrolled impacts safe.

Section 3: Playing Area

Use a level, unobstructed area with a forgiving surface, such as well-maintained grass or an appropriate indoor activity surface. Do not play near traffic, walls, fences, trees, playground structures, holes, wet patches, loose gravel, ice, or other hazards.

Team Lines

The teams form parallel lines facing one another. For a typical recreational game, the lines should be about 6 to 9 metres (20 to 30 feet) apart. Younger players, beginners, and groups using a no-impact variation should use the shorter end of that range. The game leader may reduce the distance further to keep running speeds low.

Each line needs enough width for all team members to stand comfortably without stretching, crowding, or pulling one another sideways. Leave several metres of clear runoff space behind each line and at both ends so a runner can slow down safely.

Boundaries

If boundaries are used, establish them before play. A runner who deliberately leaves the agreed area to gain an advantage has an unsuccessful attempt. Minor accidental steps outside a boundary may be ignored when they do not affect the challenge.

Environmental Conditions

Stop play when visibility is poor or when heat, cold, rain, wind, or surface conditions make running or stopping unsafe. Indoors, allow extra clearance from walls and fixed objects. The playing area should be rechecked whenever conditions change.

Section 4: Players & Officials

Teams

Red Rover works best with at least four players on each team. Divide participants as evenly as possible, considering age, size, speed, and mobility rather than relying only on equal numbers. If an odd player remains, one team may begin with the extra player or the game leader may rotate that player between teams.

Each team forms one continuous line. In the classic version, adjacent players hold hands or use another agreed hand-to-hand link. Players may not wrap arms around one another, lock wrists, grip clothing, or use an interlocking hold that cannot be released immediately.

Game Leader

A responsible game leader is strongly recommended and should be required for children. The leader establishes the variation, balances the teams, identifies valid line positions, alternates turns, resolves uncertainty, and stops unsafe conduct. The leader's safety decisions are final.

Callers and Runners

On each turn, the active team selects one caller to deliver the traditional invitation: “Red Rover, Red Rover, send [player's name] right over.” Any clear wording that identifies exactly one opposing player is acceptable.

The named player becomes the runner for that turn. A player may decline a run without penalty or explanation; the calling team then names another willing player. To keep everyone involved, teams should avoid repeatedly selecting the same runner when other willing players have had fewer turns.

Substitutions and Accessibility

Players may enter or leave between turns with the leader's approval. The teams should be rebalanced when necessary. Groups should adapt the challenge to participants' abilities—for example, by using walking, tagging, or a cooperative skill task—without treating an accommodation as a competitive disadvantage.

Section 5: Rules of Play

Starting the Game

  1. Agree on the classic or no-break format, the winning condition, boundaries, and any house rules.
  2. Divide the players into two balanced teams.
  3. Each team forms a line facing the other team.
  4. Choose the first calling team by mutual agreement or a simple chance method.

Classic Turn Sequence

  1. The active team clearly calls one willing player from the opposing team.
  2. The called player waits until the game leader signals that both teams are ready.
  3. The runner travels directly toward one chosen link between two defenders.
  4. The attempt ends when the link clearly separates, the runner is safely stopped without passing through, the runner stops voluntarily, or the leader calls the play dead.
  5. If the runner passes completely through the separated link under the agreed standard, the attempt succeeds. The runner returns to the original team with one of the two players whose link was challenged. The successful runner chooses which one.
  6. If the link remains intact and the runner does not pass through, the attempt is unsuccessful. The runner joins the defending team.
  7. The other team becomes the calling team, regardless of the result.

A link must be established before the ready signal and may not be moved to surprise the runner. Defenders should face forward and remain near their line. They may release immediately for safety; doing so makes the runner successful but should never be discouraged.

Recommended No-Break Format

For a lower-risk game, do not form a physical chain. Defenders stand side by side with visible gaps and hands kept to themselves. The called player approaches under control and completes a predetermined non-contact challenge, such as reaching the line before a spoken count ends or passing through a gap selected before the run.

For a simple no-break challenge, the calling team secretly chooses a gap, the runner names a gap, and the choice is revealed. A match is a successful attempt; a non-match is unsuccessful. Apply the same team-transfer results as in classic play. This preserves calling, anticipation, and team movement without collisions or force against linked arms.

Common House Rules

  • Choice of defender: After breaking a link, the runner may take either adjacent defender, or the group may require the nearer defender to transfer.
  • Rescue rule: A successful runner may retrieve any former teammate instead of one of the challenged defenders.
  • Walking Red Rover: All runners must walk briskly and must keep one foot in contact with the ground.
  • Three-step challenge: The runner begins only three large steps from the line, reducing speed and impact.
  • Tag-the-gap: Defenders do not link. The runner scores by tagging a called gap or designated point while defenders remain stationary.
  • No transfers: Teams remain fixed and record points for successful defenses and successful runs.

Section 6: Scoring

Traditional Team-Transfer Game

The traditional game does not require numerical scoring. Players move between teams after each challenge, and the game ends when one team has gained all players. That team wins.

If time expires first, the team with more players wins. If the teams have equal numbers, the result is a draw unless the group agreed in advance to play one additional turn per team.

Point-Scoring Option

For a fixed-team game, award one point to the runner's team for a successful challenge and one point to the defending team for an unsuccessful challenge. Each team must receive the same number of turns. The team with the higher total wins.

A practical match consists of three to five calling turns per team. If the score is tied, declare a draw or play paired extra turns, with each team receiving one turn, until one team leads after a complete pair.

Short-Game Option

The group may set a target before play, such as winning when one team reaches two-thirds of all players. This avoids continuing until only one participant remains on the smaller team. State how fractions will be rounded before the game begins.

Fair Results

No point or transfer should reward dangerous behavior. If a challenge is stopped for safety, replay the turn unless one side clearly caused the unsafe situation. When teams become badly mismatched, the leader may end the game, rebalance the sides, and start a new round.

Section 7: Violations & Penalties

Runner Violations

  • Starting before the ready signal.
  • Changing to a different link after committing to the selected challenge area when the house rules require advance selection.
  • Diving, leaping feet-first, lowering the head or shoulder, or deliberately charging a defender.
  • Striking, pushing, kicking, grabbing, or using the hands or elbows to force a link apart.
  • Continuing after the leader calls stop or after a defender releases for safety.

A minor early start normally produces a restart. A dangerous or deliberate runner violation makes the attempt unsuccessful. The player may be removed from the round if the conduct is repeated or severe.

Defender Violations

  • Wrapping arms, locking wrists, gripping clothing, or using an unsafe hold.
  • Raising linked hands toward the runner's neck or head.
  • Stepping forward to collide with, trip, hip-check, or strike the runner.
  • Closing an agreed gap after the attempt begins or moving sideways to obstruct a runner outside the chosen link.
  • Refusing to release when continued resistance creates an obvious risk of injury.

A defender violation normally makes the run successful. In a transfer game, the runner may take one of the responsible defenders. If responsibility is unclear, replay the turn with a different challenge or use the no-break format.

Team and Conduct Violations

Calling an unwilling player repeatedly, taunting, discriminatory conduct, deliberate mismatching, or pressuring someone to participate is prohibited. The first minor incident should receive a warning; repeated or serious misconduct may result in loss of the turn, removal from the round, team rebalancing, or termination of the game.

Unclear Outcomes

If the leader cannot determine whether a link broke before the runner stopped, the turn should be replayed. Safety takes priority over assigning blame or preserving a competitive result.

Section 8: Safety Considerations

The classic form of Red Rover can cause falls, collisions, arm and shoulder injuries, and impacts to the head or neck. Many schools and youth programs therefore avoid the traditional chain-breaking version. The recommended recreational choice is the no-break format described in Section 5.

Before Play

  • Obtain appropriate adult supervision for children and explain the stop signal.
  • Inspect the surface and surrounding runoff area for obstacles, holes, slippery patches, and hard objects.
  • Balance teams by size, age, speed, and ability.
  • Choose a low-impact variation whenever players differ greatly in size or physical confidence.
  • Allow every participant to opt out, walk, or use an adapted challenge without pressure.
  • Warm up with light movement and controlled changes of direction.

During Play

Runners must remain upright, keep their heads up, and approach under control. They must not sprint at maximum speed, dive, or use any part of the body as a battering ram. Defenders must never create a link at neck height or brace against the runner with locked elbows.

Handholds, if used at all, should be light and immediately releasable. Releasing to avoid injury is always correct and counts only as the ordinary game result—not as cowardice or misconduct. No one may reinforce a link by holding a teammate with both hands or attaching to clothing.

The leader should stop the game at the first sign of unsafe speed, escalating force, fatigue, distress, poor footing, or loss of control. Do not resume until the hazard has been corrected; switch to a no-contact variation when appropriate.

Injury Response

Stop play immediately if a participant falls awkwardly, reports pain, appears dizzy or confused, has difficulty moving a limb, or receives an impact to the head or neck. Do not move a player who may have a serious head, neck, back, or limb injury unless remaining in place creates greater danger. Seek qualified medical assistance as appropriate.

A player with a suspected concussion must not return to play that day and should be evaluated under applicable local medical guidance. Any player who is injured, frightened, or uncomfortable may leave the game without penalty.

Safest Default

When there is any doubt about whether classic Red Rover is suitable, use the no-break version. The calling chant, alternating turns, teamwork, and player-transfer strategy can all be retained without asking anyone to run into joined arms.
