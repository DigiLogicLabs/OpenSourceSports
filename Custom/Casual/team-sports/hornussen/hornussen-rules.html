Section 1: Introduction

Hornussen is an indigenous Swiss sport played by two teams of 16 to 20 players which alternate in striking a puck known as a "Hornuss" (hornet) or "Nouss" and attempting to deflect it. When hit, the Hornuss can fly at up to 300 km/h and create a buzzing sound.

Section 2: Equipment

Hornussen needs four purpose-built pieces of kit, and none of them have an everyday substitute — the striking side and the fielding side use entirely different equipment from one another.

  • Hornuss (the puck): The projectile the whole game is built around, weighing roughly 78 grams. It takes its name from the buzzing hornet sound it makes in flight. Originally turned from cow horn — hence both the name and the sport's — it is now moulded from hard plastic. Struck well, it leaves the ramp at up to 300 km/h (about 185 mph).
  • Stecken (the striking club): A long, whippy rod of roughly 2–3 metres, tipped with a dense wooden or composite head called the Träf. It is not swung like a bat but whipped, so the flex of the shaft does the work; traditional shafts were wood, and carbon fibre is now normal in competition.
  • Bock (the launching ramp): A curved metal ramp that holds the Hornuss at the striking end. The Hornuss rests on the ramp rather than being thrown or teed by hand, so every strike starts from the same position.
  • Schindel (the interception board): The fielding side's equipment — a flat board of about one square metre fitted with a handle, which players hurl spinning into the air to knock the incoming Hornuss down before it lands. Each fielder carries their own.

The game is played on a long field known as the Ries; see Section 3.

Section 3: Playing Area

Hornussen is played on the Ries — a long, roughly rectangular field, typically an ordinary hayfield or meadow rather than a purpose-built stadium. It is unusually long for a team sport, running several hundred metres away from the striking end, because a well-struck Hornuss carries that far.

  • The striking end: One short end holds the Bock, the ramp the Hornuss is struck from. Everything else happens down-field of it, so the whole playing area fans out in a single direction rather than being shared by both sides at once.
  • Numbered zones: The Ries is marked out in numbered bands running away from the striking point. Where an un-intercepted Hornuss first touches down decides how much is recorded against the fielding side — the further it flies, the deeper the band and the worse the mark. This is why distance matters even though the striking team scores no points of its own.
  • The fielding side's ground: The defending team spreads out along the length of the Ries rather than holding fixed positions, because they cannot know where a strike will travel until it is in the air. Placement is a judgement about the striker.

Because the field is ordinary farmland, dimensions vary between clubs and the boundaries are marked rather than fenced. The exact regulation length, width and zone spacing are not pinned down here — see Section 6.

Section 4: Players & Officials

Two teams of 16 to 20 players face each other, and — unusually — the whole team changes role at once. There are no specialist positions in the way a football team has them: every player strikes when their side is striking, and every player fields when it is not.

  • Strikers: Each player on the striking side takes their own turn at the Bock before the innings changes over. The whip of the Stecken takes years to learn, so clubs tend to carry a wide spread of ability and the order matters.
  • Fielders: The same players, once the roles swap, spread down the Ries with their Schindeln. Reading the flight of a small, fast, high puck is the core defensive skill, and the sport is followed as closely for a good interception as for a long strike.
  • Officials: The decisions that must be made are narrow but consequential — whether a Hornuss was brought down before it landed, and which numbered zone it first touched. Both feed straight into what is recorded against the fielding team, so both are judged on the field as play proceeds.

Hornussen is a club and village sport, amateur and strongly regional — it is concentrated in the Bernese Mittelland and Emmental, and clubs compete within cantonal and federal associations.

Section 5: Rules of Play

Hornussen is played in alternating innings. One team strikes while the other defends the field, then the roles swap. Unusually, the two sides use completely different equipment and do completely different things — the striking side is trying to send the Hornuss as far down the field as it can, and the fielding side is trying to knock it out of the air before it lands.

Striking

  • The Hornuss is placed on the Bock, the curved metal ramp at the head of the field, so every strike begins from the same position.
  • The striker takes a run-up and swings the Stecken in a long overhead arc, whipping the Träf head down onto the Hornuss. The power comes from the flex of the shaft rather than from arm strength, which is why the technique takes so long to learn.
  • A well-struck Hornuss leaves the ramp at up to 300 km/h (about 185 mph) and can carry several hundred metres down the Ries.
  • Every player on the striking team takes their turn before the innings changes over.

Defending

  • The fielding team spreads out across the length of the Ries, each player holding a Schindel — the handled board.
  • As the Hornuss comes in, fielders hurl their Schindel spinning up into its flight path to bring it down. Judging the line of a small, fast, high-flying puck is the core skill of the fielding side, and it is why the sport is followed as closely for its defence as for its hitting.
  • A Hornuss brought down before it touches the ground is stopped, and no mark is recorded against the fielding team.

The objective

A Hornuss that lands in the Ries without being intercepted is recorded as a Nouss against the fielding team. This is the part that surprises newcomers: the marks accumulate against the side that failed to stop it, not in favour of the side that struck it, so the winning team is the one with the fewest Nouss rather than the most points. How far the Hornuss travels matters too — the Ries is marked out in numbered zones, and a strike that lands deeper in the field is worth more against the defenders than a short one.

The exact zone values, innings count and match length are still being pinned down — see Section 6, and propose them if you play.
