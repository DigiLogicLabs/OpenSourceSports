Section 1: Introduction

Go Fish is a children's matching game and usually the first card game anyone learns. Players ask each other for cards of a particular rank, trying to collect all four of a kind — a book. Ask someone who does not have it and they tell you to "go fish".

It is traditional, in the public domain, and deliberately simple: there is no bluffing requirement, no betting, and no penalty for a wrong guess beyond losing your turn.

Section 2: The Deck

  • One standard 52-card deck, no jokers.
  • Only rank matters — suits are irrelevant.
  • A book is all four cards of one rank.
↑ Back to Contents

Section 3: Setup & Deal

  • Two or three players: 7 cards each. Four or more: 5 each.
  • The rest becomes the pond (stock), spread face down.
  • Play begins to the dealer's left.

Section 4: Gameplay

Asking

  • On your turn, ask one specific player for one specific rank — "Marco, do you have any sevens?"
  • You must already hold at least one card of that rank. This is the rule most often forgotten.

The Two Outcomes

  • They have it: they hand over every card of that rank, and you go again.
  • They do not: they say "go fish". You draw one card from the pond and your turn ends — unless you draw exactly the rank you asked for, in which case many households let you go again.

Books

Whenever you hold all four of a rank, lay the book face up in front of you immediately.

Running Out

If you run out of cards, draw a new hand from the pond if any remain. The game ends when all thirteen books have been made.

Section 5: Scoring & Winning

Each completed book is worth one point. There are thirteen in the deck, so ties are possible only with an even split among players.

The player with the most books wins.

Section 6: Variants & House Rules

  • Pairs instead of books: collect two of a rank rather than four — much faster, better for very young players.
  • Ask anyone: some versions drop the "must hold that rank" requirement, which makes the game almost pure chance.
  • Literature / Canadian Fish: the serious adult relatives, played in teams with strict deduction and no drawing.
  • Themed decks: commercial children's versions replace ranks with pictures; the rules are unchanged.
