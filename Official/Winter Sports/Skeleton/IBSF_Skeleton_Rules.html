Section 1: Introduction

1.1 Overview and Governing Body

Skeleton is a head-first, prone-position sliding sport in which athletes race individually down an ice track on a small sled, reaching speeds exceeding 140 km/h (87 mph) and experiencing G-forces up to 5G in curves. Governed by the International Bobsleigh and Skeleton Federation (IBSF) under the IBSF International Rules for Skeleton, the sport returned to the Olympic program at the 2002 Salt Lake City Games after a 54-year absence (previously contested only at St. Moritz in 1928 and 1948). Both men’s and women’s individual events are contested at the Olympics.

1.2 History and Olympic Status

Skeleton originated in St. Moritz, Switzerland, in the late 19th century on the famous Cresta Run, a natural ice track built in 1884. The name “skeleton” likely derives from the stripped-down, bony appearance of the early metal sleds. After appearing at the 1928 and 1948 Olympics (both held in St. Moritz, the only venue with a suitable track at the time), the sport was absent from the Games until its permanent return in 2002. The addition of women’s skeleton in 2002 marked one of the first times a women’s sliding event was included in the Olympics.

1.3 Distinction from Luge and Bobsled

  • Skeleton: Athlete lies face-down (prone), head-first on a flat sled. Sprint start with a running push. Steers with body weight, shoulders, and toe spikes.
  • Luge: Athlete lies face-up (supine), feet-first on a sled. Seated start from handles. Steers with legs and shoulders against runners.
  • Bobsled: Multi-person crew sits upright in an enclosed sled. Driver steers with rope pulleys connected to the front runners. Running push start.
  • All three sports share the same artificially refrigerated tracks but use different start positions, sled designs, and technique.

Section 2: Equipment

2.1 The Sled

  • Maximum sled weight: 45 kg (99.2 lb) for men, 38 kg (83.8 lb) for women (Art. 10.14, 12.2)
  • Maximum combined weight (sled + athlete with complete race equipment): 120 kg (264.6 lb) for men, 102 kg (224.9 lb) for women (Art. 10.14). Ballast may be added to reach the limit, but only fixed securely to the frame, base plate or saddle; ballast on the athlete’s body is prohibited.
  • Sled dimensions: Length 800–1,250 mm (31.5–49.2 in); overall height 80–200 mm (3.1–7.9 in) as prepared for the race (Art. 12.3). The 2026 edition raised the maximum length from 1,200 mm.
  • Runners: Two steel runners with a gauge of 340–380 mm (13.4–15 in), measured runner centre to runner centre (Art. 12.3). Runner profile must match IBSF specifications (Art. 12.8).
  • Frame: Steel or composite frame. Handle bumpers (padded protrusions on the front) for the athlete to grip during the start push. No mechanical steering mechanisms — no moving parts.
  • Sled identification: Each sled is registered with the IBSF and marked with a unique identification number. Sleds are weighed and inspected before each race.

2.2 Helmet

  • A safety helmet is mandatory in all training and races; its shell and padding must cover the head and at least the ear area, and National Federations are responsible for safety-standard compliance (Art. 10.16.1)
  • Helmets with spoilers or protruding edges are not allowed; the chin guard, visor and their fixings are the only permitted protrusions (Art. 10.16.1)
  • Visor or goggles may be used to protect against ice spray and wind
  • Helmet must be securely fastened with a chin strap at all times during a run

2.3 Suit and Shoes

  • Speed suit: Skin-tight racing suit made of approved materials. Maximum material thickness regulated by IBSF. No aerodynamic aids, ribbing, or surface texturing beyond normal fabric weave.
  • Spike shoes: Purpose-built shoes with brush-form spikes on the sole for traction during the running start. Spikes may be at most 5 mm (0.2 in) long and 1.5 mm in diameter, with at least 250 spikes per shoe (Art. 10.16.2). Spikes are also used for minor steering adjustments during the run (toe pressure on the ice).

2.4 Runner Temperature Regulations

  • Runner temperature is measured with an IBSF-approved device before each heat (Art. 10.11)
  • The runners mounted on the sled may be at most 4 °C warmer than the reference runner; if the reference runner is below -14 °C, the sled’s runners may be at most -10 °C (Art. 10.11)
  • The reference runner is set out in the open air 45 minutes before the race (Art. 10.12)
  • If a reading exceeds the limit, both runners are measured again immediately; a second reading over the limit means immediate disqualification (Art. 10.11)

Section 3: Playing Area

3.1 Track Specifications

  • Track length: 1200–1650 m (3937–5413 ft) from the skeleton/bobsled start to the finish
  • Vertical drop: 100–150 m (328–492 ft) from start to finish
  • Number of curves: 15–20 curves, including S-curves, hairpins, and high-banked turns
  • Average gradient: 8–15% overall; sections may reach 20% on steeper passages
  • Tracks are shared with bobsled and luge competitions (skeleton and bobsled use the same start; luge starts lower)

3.2 Track Construction

  • Artificially refrigerated: ammonia or Freon-based refrigeration coils embedded in the concrete track structure maintain ice at -5 °C to -8 °C (23–18 °F)
  • Ice surface: 2.5–5 cm (1–2 in) thick, built up by hand-spraying water layers and shaped by track crews
  • Track walls: raised concrete walls lined with ice; roofed sections on some modern tracks to protect from sun and precipitation
  • Only 16 artificially refrigerated sliding tracks exist worldwide (as of 2026), making track access a limiting factor for the sport’s growth

3.3 Start Area

  • Running start zone: Approximately 30–40 m (98–131 ft) of level or slightly downhill ice surface
  • Start block/handles at the beginning of the start zone for athletes to position their sled
  • Start timing: The start time is measured at the 50 m light beam (Art. 10.6.15)
  • Start area is grooved or textured for spike shoe traction

3.4 Finish Area

  • Electronic timing beam at the finish line records time to 1/100th of a second
  • Deceleration zone (braking stretch) after the finish: minimum 100 m of uphill track for the sled to slow naturally
  • Foam padding on the terminal wall at the end of the braking stretch
  • Sled recovery area where athletes exit the track and equipment is collected for post-race inspection

Section 4: Players & Officials

4.1 Athlete Qualifications

  • Men’s and women’s skeleton are individual events. IBSF also runs a Skeleton Mixed Team of one woman and one man from the same National Federation, each sliding one heat; both must be registered and qualified for the individual races (Art. 4.7, 10.6.1)
  • Athletes must hold a valid IBSF license and be registered by their national federation
  • The minimum age for an IBSF International License is 13; athletes under 18 are Youth athletes and need their parents’ permission (Art. 8.4)
  • Every athlete must complete two training heats without accident to race (Art. 10.1.5). Olympic Winter Games and World Championships offer 6 official training heats; World Cup and Continental Championships offer at least 6 over 2 days (Art. 10.1.1–10.1.2)
  • Olympic qualification criteria are set by the IOC (Art. 4.7)

4.2 Officials and Jury

  • Jury: IBSF Technical Delegate, Race Director, Jury President — oversee all aspects of competition
  • Start officials: Verify start procedure, confirm athlete and sled positioning, control start signal
  • Track officials: Positioned at key curves to monitor athlete passage and report incidents via radio
  • Equipment controllers: Weigh sled and athlete immediately after a heat when the Jury requests it (Art. 10.14); check runner temperatures; inspect sled compliance
  • Timekeepers: Electronic timing to 1/100th of a second, with a start time at 50 m and four evenly distributed intermediate times and four speed measurements (Art. 10.6.15)
  • Video officials: Review footage from track cameras for rule violations, interference, or safety incidents

4.3 Start Order

  • First heat: the 10 best-placed athletes in the IBSF Discipline Ranking choose start numbers 1–10; the rest start from 11 in ranking order (Art. 10.6.3)
  • Four-heat races: heat 2 runs 25 to 1 and then 26 to the end on the heat-1 ranking; heat 3 runs 1 to the end on the standings after two heats; heat 4 runs 25 to 1 on the standings after three heats (Art. 10.6.2)
  • World Cup second heat: 20 to 1, on the ranking after the first heat (Art. 10.6.2)
  • The Jury may reduce the number of participants before each subsequent heat (Art. 10.6.10)
  • After the starter clears the track, the athlete must activate the timing within 30 seconds (Art. 10.6.8)

Section 5: Rules of Play

5.1 Competition Format

  • Olympic Winter Games and World Championships: in principle 4 heats (Art. 10.6.1). Combined cumulative time of all heats determines the final ranking.
  • Continental Championships and all other official IBSF races, including the World Cup: 2 heats (Art. 10.6.1)
  • At least one heat must be completed for a race to be officially valid (Art. 10.6.1)
  • Training: see 4.1; each athlete needs two accident-free training heats to race (Art. 10.1.5)

5.2 The Start

  • Athlete positions the sled at the start block, gripping the front handles (bumpers)
  • On the start signal (green light or audible tone), the athlete sprints while pushing the sled for 30–40 m
  • After building maximum speed, the athlete dives head-first, face-down (prone) onto the sled
  • The start is the only phase where the athlete can generate speed — after loading, momentum comes entirely from gravity
  • Start time (measured over approximately 50 m) is a critical performance differentiator; top athletes complete the push phase in under 5 seconds

5.3 Sliding and Steering

  • Athletes steer by shifting body weight: pressing the right shoulder into the sled to turn left, and vice versa
  • Knee pressure against the sled frame provides additional directional control
  • Toe spikes dragged lightly on the ice surface create friction on one side for fine steering adjustments
  • Head position affects aerodynamics; athletes keep their chin tucked and body streamlined
  • Arms are held tight alongside the body or sled after loading; arms dragging on ice results in friction and time loss
  • No mechanical steering devices, brakes, or moving parts on the sled are permitted

5.4 Finishing

  • Only the head-first prone position is allowed, and after timing starts at the 50 m point the athlete may not lose contact with the sled (Art. 10.6.5)
  • Athlete must cross the finish beam on the sled, and the beam must be broken by the athlete’s helmet (Art. 10.6.5)
  • After a crash, the athlete is ranked if they cross the finish beam in contact with the sled (Art. 10.6.5)
  • After crossing the finish, the athlete rides into the deceleration zone and uses toe spikes and body drag to slow the sled

5.5 Equipment Inspection Between Runs

  • Sled and athlete are weighed immediately after a heat on the Jury’s request, with no modification allowed beforehand; the athlete may request a second weighing and has up to 5 minutes to clear snow and ice first (Art. 10.14)
  • Sled dimensions and runner profiles may be checked against IBSF templates at any point during competition
  • Any modification to the sled between inspection and the start is prohibited

Section 6: Scoring

6.1 Timing and Results

  • All times recorded to 1/100th of a second (two decimal places)
  • Final ranking determined by the cumulative time of all runs (4 runs for Olympics/World Championships, 2 runs for World Cup)
  • Lowest cumulative time wins
  • Typical run times: 50–65 seconds depending on track length and conditions
  • Winning margins at Olympic level are frequently less than 0.50 seconds over 4 runs

6.2 Split Times

  • Four intermediate times are evenly distributed along the track after the 50 m start time (Art. 10.6.15)
  • Start time (0–50 m) is a key split, as the push phase is the only athlete-powered portion of the run
  • Split times help coaches and athletes analyze performance through specific track sections
  • Split time data is broadcast to spectators and television for real-time comparison

6.3 Tiebreaking

  • Times equal to 1/100th of a second are given the same rank; ties are not broken (Art. 10.6.16)
  • IBSF World Cup: tied athletes receive the same World Cup points

6.4 World Cup and Ranking Points

  • IBSF World Cup points awarded for top 40 finishers per race: 225-210-200-192-184-176-168-160-152-144 (1st–10th), continuing to 68 for 20th, 40 for 25th and 5 for 40th (Art. 11.3)
  • World ranking determines Olympic quotas and World Cup start order
  • Rankings updated after each competition based on cumulative season points

Section 7: Violations & Penalties

7.1 Equipment Violations

  • Runner temperature over the limit on a second reading: immediate disqualification (Art. 10.11)
  • Any violation of the IBSF International Rules found in technical inspection: automatic disqualification from the competition concerned (Art. 10.9)
  • Runner sanding in parc fermé: only with IBSF-provided abrasives, runners mounted on the sled; any other material or product means immediate disqualification from the race (Art. 10.7)

7.2 Procedural Violations

  • Athlete not on sled at finish: DNF (Did Not Finish) for that run; time not recorded
  • Start window: after the track is cleared, the athlete must activate the timing within 30 seconds (Art. 10.6.8)
  • Missing mandatory equipment check: Athlete may not start the run until inspection is completed

7.3 Protests

  • A protest must be made verbally to the Jury immediately, or at most 5 minutes after the heat in which the incident happened, and its grounds submitted in writing no more than 20 minutes after the race ends; without the verbal protest no written protest is accepted (Art. 10.19)
  • Only the team captain may protest; collective protests are not allowed (Art. 10.19)
  • A €100 fee accompanies the written protest and is forfeited if the protest is rejected (Art. 10.19)
  • The Jury decides by simple majority, with the Jury President casting the deciding vote, and issues a written decision; it is final and takes effect immediately (Art. 10.19)

7.4 Conduct Violations

  • Unsportsmanlike behavior (verbal abuse of officials, damaging track infrastructure): warning, fine, or suspension
  • Interference with another athlete’s equipment or sled: DSQ and potential IBSF disciplinary proceedings
  • Doping violations handled per WADA code: provisional suspension, results invalidation, and IBSF disciplinary action

Section 8: Safety Considerations

8.1 Personal Protective Equipment

  • Chin-guard helmet: Mandatory for all runs (training and competition). Must provide face and chin protection since the athlete’s head is the leading body part, centimeters from the ice surface.
  • Speed suit: Provides some abrasion protection in case of a crash or sled ejection
  • Spike shoes: Must be worn; provide traction during the start push and braking control during the finish deceleration
  • No additional body armor or padding is standard in skeleton (unlike bobsled pilots who may wear additional protection)

8.2 Track Safety Features

  • Track walls padded with foam at high-impact zones (tight curves, S-bends, and areas where ejections are most likely)
  • Netting or catch fences installed above walls at curves where athletes have historically left the track
  • Ice surface inspected and maintained by track crews between every 5–10 athletes; cracks, debris, and uneven surfaces repaired
  • Speed monitoring at multiple points along the track; if speeds exceed design parameters, the start gate may be lowered (moving the start further down the in-run)

8.3 Medical and Emergency Protocols

  • Medical team stationed at the finish area with ambulance and emergency equipment
  • Track marshals at every curve with radio communication to race control and medical team
  • Crash protocol: if an athlete crashes, competition is immediately halted until the athlete is cleared or evacuated, the sled is recovered, and the track is inspected and repaired
  • Concussion protocol: any athlete involved in a crash is assessed for concussion before being allowed to take subsequent runs
  • Nearest hospital and helicopter evacuation route identified in the competition safety plan

8.4 Training and Progression Requirements

  • New athletes must complete a progression program starting from lower start positions before being permitted to use the full-length start
  • Each athlete must complete two training heats without accident before racing (Art. 10.1.5); this requirement accounts for each track’s unique curve profiles and speeds
  • Track orientation sessions conducted by experienced pilots or IBSF officials for athletes new to a particular track
