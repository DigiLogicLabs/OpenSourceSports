1. Introduction

Go is a game of strategy between two sides, usually played on a 19x19 grid (AGA Rule 1). Known as baduk in Korea and weiqi in China, it is one of the oldest board games still played under codified rules.

Players alternate placing stones on the intersections of the grid, aiming to control more of the board than the opponent. The object of the game is to end with the greater total number of points (AGA Rule 12).

These are the rules of the American Go Association, adopted April 1991. The AGA rule set is designed so that a game scored by territory and the same game scored by area produce the same result — a property most other rule sets do not guarantee (AGA Rule 12).

2. Components

The Board

  • A grid of 19x19 lines, giving 361 playing points where lines intersect (AGA Rule 1)
  • Smaller boards are permitted, 13x13 and 9x9 being the two most common (AGA Rule 1)
  • Nine marked points (star points or hoshi) are used to place handicap stones (AGA Rule 4)

The Stones

  • Two sets of lens-shaped stones, one black and one white (AGA Rule 1)
  • A full set is 181 black and 180 white — enough to fill all 361 points, with Black holding the extra stone as Black plays first
  • Stones are placed on the intersections, including edges and corners, never inside the squares (AGA Rule 1)

Other Equipment

  • Two bowls or lids to hold stones and captured prisoners
  • Pass stones — under AGA rules a player who passes hands the opponent a stone, which counts as a prisoner (AGA Rule 7)
  • A game clock for timed and tournament play

3. Setup

Starting Position

  • The board starts empty; there is no initial arrangement of stones in an even game (AGA Rule 1)
  • Black moves first in an even game (AGA Rule 2)

Komi (Compensation)

  • Because Black plays first, White receives komi (AGA Rule 3) — compensation added to White's score
  • AGA komi in an even game is 7½ points. The AGA raised komi from 5½ in August 2004, effective 2005; the half point prevents draws (AGA Rule 3)
  • In a handicap game White instead receives ½ point, again to avoid a tie (AGA Rule 3)

Handicap

  • The weaker player takes Black (AGA Rule 4)
  • Black either moves first normally — a one-stone handicap, giving White only ½ point — or places 2 to 9 stones on the board before White's first move (AGA Rule 4)
  • Handicap games are the standard way for players of different ranks to play an even contest

4. Gameplay

Making a Move

  • Players alternate. A move is either placing one stone of your colour on any empty intersection, or passing (AGA Rule 2)
  • Passing is always legal, even when legal placements exist (AGA Rule 2)
  • Stones do not move once placed — they are only removed by capture (AGA Rule 5)

Liberties, Strings & Capture

  • Adjacent stones of the same colour form a string and share their liberties (AGA Rule 5)
  • A liberty is an empty point orthogonally adjacent to a stone in the string (AGA Rule 5)
  • A string with no remaining liberties is captured and removed from the board as prisoners (AGA Rule 5)
  • Suicide is illegal — you may not play a stone that would leave your own string without liberties, unless the play captures first (AGA Rule 5)

The Ko Rule

  • It is illegal to play so as to recreate a previous board position from the game with the same player to move (AGA Rule 6)
  • This is a positional superko: it covers the classic single-point ko and also longer repeating cycles (AGA Rule 6)
  • In practice a player facing a ko must play elsewhere (a ko threat) before recapturing

Passing

  • A player who passes gives the opponent a pass stone, which counts as a prisoner (AGA Rule 7)
  • Pass stones — handed to the opponent when passing — are what keep AGA territory scoring and area scoring in agreement (AGA Rules 7, 12)
  • White must make the last move, an additional pass if necessary, so both players have played or passed an equal number of stones (AGA Rule 11)

5. Scoring & Winning

Ending the Game

  • Two consecutive passes normally signal the end of the game (AGA Rule 9)
  • The players then agree which stones on the board are dead (AGA Rule 9) — stones that could not avoid capture if play continued
  • Dead stones are removed and counted as prisoners; if the players cannot agree, play resumes to settle the position (AGA Rule 10)

Counting the Score

  • AGA rules permit either method, chosen before the game, and both give the same result (AGA Rule 12):
  • Territory scoring: empty points you have surrounded, plus prisoners you hold (AGA Rule 12)
  • Area scoring: your stones on the board, plus the empty points you have surrounded (AGA Rule 12)
  • White's komi is added to White's total (AGA Rules 3, 12)

The Result

  • The player with the greater total is the winner (AGA Rule 12)
  • The half point in komi means an even game cannot end in a draw (AGA Rule 3)
  • Results are conventionally recorded as a margin, e.g. "B+3.5" — Black by three and a half points

6. Variants & House Rules

Board Sizes

  • 13x13 and 9x9 are the common smaller boards, standard for teaching and fast games (AGA Rule 1)
  • Smaller boards — 13x13 and 9x9 — are permitted, and a non-integral komi should still be used there (AGA Rules 1, 3), since avoiding a drawn game is part of the spirit of the AGA rules

Other Rule Sets

  • Japanese rules use territory scoring and treat ko and life-and-death differently in rare positions
  • Chinese rules use area scoring and a different komi convention
  • The differences change the result only in unusual endgames, but tournaments always announce which rule set applies

Common Tournament Conventions

  • Handicap stones let players of different ranks meet on even terms (AGA Rule 4) — one stone per rank difference is the usual guide
  • Timed games use byo-yomi (reserve periods) or Fischer increment; time is a tournament regulation, not part of the rules of play
