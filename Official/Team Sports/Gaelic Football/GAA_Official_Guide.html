Section 1: Introduction

Gaelic football is one of the Gaelic games administered by the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA), the amateur sporting and cultural organisation founded in Thurles in 1884. The sport is played by 15-a-side teams who advance a round football toward an opponent's goal by a combination of kicking, hand-passing, and carrying, scoring by sending the ball either over the crossbar for a point or under it into the net for a goal. The definitive rules of the game are set out in the GAA's Official Guide - Part 2, which contains the Playing Rules of Football alongside the Playing Rules of Hurling.

The rules referenced in this document reflect the Official Guide Part 2 (15.04.2026 edition), which incorporates the most significant overhaul of the playing rules in the game's modern history. Following a two-year review by the GAA's Football Review Committee (FRC), a package of rule enhancements was trialled on a temporary basis throughout the 2025 club and inter-county season. At Special Congress on 4 October 2025, delegates voted to ratify the enhancements - with some modifications arising from the FRC's Final Report - as permanent rule, effective from 1 January 2026. The 2026 Allianz Football League was the first full competition played under the permanent rule set. Headline changes include the introduction of a two-point score from an arc 40 metres from goal, a mandatory minimum of three outfield players in each team's own half of the field, restructured kickout and throw-in procedures, an expanded black-card sanction, and the use of a centralised game clock and hooter to signal the end of each half.

This rulebook covers the senior adult grade of Gaelic football as governed by the GAA. Distinct rule variations exist for underage grades (e.g. reduced pitch sizes, shortened playing time) and for Ladies Gaelic Football, which is governed separately by the Ladies Gaelic Football Association (LGFA) under its own rulebook; those variations are outside the scope of this document.

Section 2: Equipment

The football. Per the Official Guide Part 2 specification for the football (the same ball code is shared with other Gaelic-games specifications for round-ball ball weight and circumference), the match ball must weigh not less than 480 grams and not more than 500 grams, and must have a circumference of not less than 68 centimetres and not more than 70 centimetres. It is a round, valve-inflated leather or synthetic-leather ball (commonly a size 5 match ball in adult competition) designed to be kicked, hand-passed, and carried in the manner set out in the Playing Rules.

Playing gear. Each player is required to wear the jersey of their registered club or county, bearing an identifying number on the back, along with shorts and socks in the team's registered colours, as specified under the Playing Gear provisions of the Official Guide. Players must wear footwear suitable for the playing surface; boots with studs are standard for grass pitches. Referees have the authority to prohibit any player from taking part if their attire or any item worn is considered dangerous to themselves or to other players.

Unlike hurling, in which protective headgear meeting the relevant safety standard is mandatory for all players, Gaelic football carries no mandatory helmet or headgear requirement. Mouthguards are widely recommended by county boards and medical advisory panels but are not a mandatory playing-rule requirement for adult inter-county or club football.

Section 3: Playing Area

Field of play. The pitch is rectangular, with a length of not less than 130 metres and not more than 145 metres, and a width of not less than 80 metres and not more than 90 metres, as set out in the Field of Play specification of the Official Guide Part 2. The field is bounded by sidelines along the length and end lines (goal lines) along the width, with a halfway line marked across the pitch equidistant between the two end lines.

Goals. At the centre of each end line stands a set of goalposts consisting of two upright posts joined by a crossbar. The posts are 6.5 metres apart, and the crossbar is fixed 2.5 metres above ground level; the posts themselves extend to a minimum height of 7 metres above the ground. A ball played over the crossbar and between the posts scores a point (or two points, where applicable); a ball played under the crossbar, between the posts, and fully over the goal line scores a goal.

Pitch markings. Lines are marked parallel to each end line at distances of 13 metres, 20 metres, and 45 metres, used to regulate kickouts, frees, and the advanced mark. The goal area (the "small rectangle") is formed by two lines 4.5 metres in length, set at right angles to the end line and positioned 3.75 metres from the inside of each goalpost, giving an overall goal area 14 metres wide by 4.5 metres deep, within which only the goalkeeper (or, where the goalkeeper is not the kickout taker, the nominated free-kick taker) may stand to take a kickout.

The 40-metre arc. As part of the permanent 2026 rule set, a semi-circular arc with a 40-metre radius, centred on the middle of each goal line, is marked on all official pitches. This arc governs the boundary for two-point scores from play or frees, and defines the minimum distance a kickout must travel before it may be played by another player.

Section 4: Players & Officials

Team composition. A team consists of fifteen players: a goalkeeper and fourteen outfield players. Teams may make up to six substitutions during normal time, with a further three substitutions permitted in extra time where extra time is played, per the substitution provisions of the Official Guide. A player who has been substituted may not return to the field of play for the remainder of the game.

The "3-up / 3-back" rule. Under the permanent 2026 rules, each team must at all times keep a minimum of three outfield players inside its own half and a minimum of three outfield players inside the opposition's half. If the goalkeeper advances beyond their own 45-metre line to join the attack, their team must still maintain the required minimum of players behind the halfway line; a breach results in a free kick to the opposition from the centre of the pitch on the halfway line.

Match officials. Games are controlled by a referee, who has sole authority to enforce the Playing Rules, keep the time, and impose sanctions. The referee is assisted by two linesmen, who monitor the sidelines and assist with foul identification, and by four umpires, positioned two at each end, who signal scores and wides to the referee. At inter-county level, games are further supported by a standby/additional match official and, in competitions using it, a Hawk-Eye or equivalent score-detection system to adjudicate marginal scores.

Team officials. Each team nominates a captain, who is the sole point of contact with the referee on behalf of the team, along with team management (manager, coaches) and medical personnel, who are permitted within the technical area but may not enter the field of play without the referee's permission.

Section 5: Rules of Play

Duration. A senior inter-county adult game lasts 70 minutes, played in two halves of 35 minutes. The majority of adult club competitions are played over 60 minutes, in two halves of 30 minutes. Games at inter-county level are managed under a centralised game clock and hooter system introduced under the 2026 permanent rules, under which the referee stops the official clock whenever the ball goes dead, and the hooter sounds to signal the end of each half once the allotted playing time has elapsed and the ball is next dead.

Playing the ball. A player may kick the ball, or strike it with a clenched fist or the open hand (a "hand-pass"), but may not throw it. The ball may be carried in the hand(s) for a maximum of four consecutive steps, after which it must be released by a kick, a hand-pass, a bounce off the ground back into the hand(s), or a "solo," in which the ball is dropped or tapped onto the foot and kicked back up into the hand(s). A player may not bounce the ball twice in succession without an intervening solo, kick, or hand-pass.

The tackle. A player in possession may be fairly challenged by a shoulder-to-shoulder charge directed at the area of the body between shoulder and hip, provided both players are moving toward the ball or are in a position to play it. Pulling or holding an opponent's jersey or body, tripping, and the "third-man tackle" (two or more players tackling simultaneously in a manner that denies the player in possession a fair opportunity to play the ball) are prohibited and penalised as fouls.

The mark. A player who catches, without the ball first touching the ground, a kick delivered from beyond the 45-metre line (in open play) or from a kickout beyond the 20-metre line, is awarded a free, known as the mark. The mark may be played as a free kick, or the player may elect to play on immediately, in which case they may carry the ball for up to four steps, or make one further play of the ball, before being fairly tackled.

Kickouts. Following a score or a wide, the defending goalkeeper (or a nominated teammate, if the goalkeeper is not taking it) restarts play with a kickout from the ground, taken from the centre of the 20-metre line. The kickout must travel beyond the 40-metre arc before it may be played by any player of either team; opposing players must remain outside the 20-metre line and 13 metres from the ball until it is kicked. A teammate of the kicker may not play the restarted ball straight back to the goalkeeper inside the small rectangle.

The "solo-and-go." Where a free is awarded outside the opposition's 20-metre line, the fouled player (or a nearby teammate) may take the free immediately by soloing the ball and playing on, without waiting for the referee's whistle, provided the foul was not accompanied by a card. Opponents must retreat at least 4 metres from the mark of the free; interference with a solo-and-go inside that zone results in the free being advanced by the referee.

Throw-in. Play begins at the start of each half with a throw-in contested between one nominated player from each team at the centre of the pitch; no other player may enter the contest for the ball until it has been played by one of the two contesting players.

Section 6: Scoring

Goal. A goal, scored when the ball passes over the goal line, under the crossbar, and between the goalposts, is worth three points.

Point. A point, scored when the ball passes over the crossbar and between the goalposts, is worth one point under ordinary circumstances.

Two-point score. Under the permanent 2026 rule, a point scored from play or from a free kick is worth two points if, at the moment the ball is struck, the kicker has at least one foot on or outside the 40-metre arc. This applies to shots taken during open play as well as to free kicks awarded from beyond the arc.

Hand-passed goals. A goal may not be scored directly from a hand-pass; the ball must be kicked, or have last been played off the foot, for a goal to be validly awarded when the final action sends the ball into the net. A point may be validly scored by a hand-pass.

Wide. A shot that passes outside either goalpost, or over the end line without passing between the posts, is recorded as a wide and results in a kickout to the defending team.

Section 7: Violations & Penalties

Technical fouls. Minor infractions of the playing rules - such as an over-carry (exceeding the permitted four steps), a double bounce, an unfair pick-up of the ball from the ground, or a "square ball" offense in the small rectangle - are penalised with a free kick to the opposing team from the place of the infringement, without further sanction.

Aggressive fouls. More serious physical infractions - including striking, tripping, pulling down an opponent, jersey-pulling, and the third-man tackle - result in a free kick to the fouled team and may additionally be sanctioned with a yellow, black, or red card at the referee's discretion, depending on severity.

Yellow card. A yellow card is a formal caution for cynical or reckless conduct. A player receiving a second yellow card in the same game is dismissed and shown a red card, and the offending team plays the remainder of the game with one fewer player, without replacement.

Black card. A black card is issued for specified cynical fouls - including but not limited to deliberately tripping or pulling down an opponent who has a goal-scoring opportunity or clear advantage, deliberately body-colliding with an opponent off the ball, and interfering with a "solo-and-go" - and results in the player being withdrawn from play for a 10-minute sin-bin period, during which the player may be replaced by a substitute. A player black-carded a second time in the same game is dismissed with a red card and is not replaced, leaving the team to finish with a numerical disadvantage.

Red card. A red card is shown for the most serious infractions (such as violent conduct, deliberate striking, or a second cautionable/black-card offense) and results in the immediate and permanent dismissal of the player, who may not be replaced.

Dissent. Dissent shown toward a match official, or interference with a fouled player's solo-and-go restart within the protected 4-metre zone, results in the referee advancing the free 50 metres toward the offending team's goal, and may additionally be sanctioned with a card.

Section 8: Safety Considerations

Legal contact. The only form of body contact permitted between opponents is the shoulder-to-shoulder charge, directed at the torso of a player who is playing, or is in a position to play, the ball. Charges directed at an opponent's back, head, or neck, charges delivered with excessive force, and challenges made when an opponent is not in a position to play the ball are prohibited as dangerous play and are penalised as aggressive fouls, typically with a card.

Dangerous tackling. The third-man tackle, the "chop" tackle around an opponent's legs, and any challenge that endangers the safety of an opponent are specifically identified as dangerous play under the Playing Rules and are subject to mandatory sanction, up to and including dismissal for reckless or deliberate endangerment.

Head injury protocols. Inter-county competitions operate a Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol, under which a player suspected of having sustained a head injury is removed from the field for assessment by team medical personnel, with a temporary substitute permitted while the assessment is carried out. A player who fails the assessment or shows signs of concussion may not return to play in that game.

Playing surface and equipment safety. The referee has authority under the Official Guide to inspect the playing surface and goalposts before a game and to prohibit play, or require remedial action, where conditions are considered unsafe. The referee may likewise prohibit any player from taking the field while wearing jewellery, casts, or other rigid items considered a danger to any player.

Qualified medical cover. Organising county boards are required to ensure that appropriately qualified first-aid or medical personnel, along with the necessary emergency equipment, are present at the venue for the duration of the game.
