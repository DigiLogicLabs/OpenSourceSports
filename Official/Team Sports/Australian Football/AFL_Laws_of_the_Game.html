Section 1: Introduction

Australian Football (also known as Australian Rules Football, or "Aussie Rules") is a contact sport played between two teams of 18 players on a large oval field, using an oval ball. The sport is governed nationally and internationally by the Australian Football League (AFL), which publishes the official Laws of Australian Football annually. This rulebook references the Laws of Australian Football 2026, the current codified rulebook maintained by the AFL Commission and administered for both the elite AFL competition and community football through affiliated state and territory bodies.

The objective of the game is to score more points than the opposition by kicking the ball between goal posts at either end of the ground. A match is played over four quarters, and the winner is the team with the higher total score at the conclusion of play, as set out in Law 3 of the Laws of Australian Football.

Australian Football traces its lineage to rules first drafted in Melbourne in 1859 by the Melbourne Football Club, making it one of the oldest codified forms of football in the world. The modern Laws of Australian Football are reviewed and amended by the AFL Commission's Laws of the Game Committee ahead of each season, with the 2026 edition incorporating the most recent interpretations and law changes ratified for AFL premiership matches.

Section 2: Equipment

Per Law 2 of the Laws of Australian Football, the football used in AFL matches must be a red or yellow (for night/wet weather matches) leather oval-shaped ball, manufactured to AFL specifications. The official match ball, supplied under the AFL's commercial agreement (currently manufactured by Sherrin), must measure between 720mm and 730mm in circumference at its long axis and between 545mm and 555mm at its short axis, with an inflation pressure of 62–76 kPa (approximately 9–11 psi), as specified in Law 2.1.

Each player must wear the club-approved playing uniform, consisting of a numbered jersey (guernsey), shorts, and socks in club colours, in accordance with Law 4 (Player Equipment). Playing numbers must be clearly displayed on the back of the jersey and, where required by competition regulations, on shorts as well, to enable identification by umpires and scorers.

Football boots (footwear) with sprigs or studs suitable for the playing surface are mandatory. Law 4 prohibits any equipment, clothing, or jewellery likely to cause injury to the wearer or another player; umpires have the authority under Law 4.2 to direct a player to remove or replace dangerous equipment before taking the field. Mouthguards are strongly recommended by the AFL and mandated at most junior and community levels under state and territory football association bylaws, though senior AFL Laws leave the mouthguard requirement to competition-specific regulations rather than the national Laws of the Game.

Goal posts and behind posts, positioned at each end of the playing surface, are fixed equipment of the ground rather than player equipment; their specifications are detailed in Section 3 (Playing Area). Padding on goal and behind posts is mandated under Law 1 for safety purposes at the level nearest the base.

Section 3: Playing Area

Under Law 1 of the Laws of Australian Football, the playing surface is an oval-shaped ground. Official AFL grounds must measure between 135m and 185m in length and between 110m and 155m in width, though most AFL premiership venues (such as the Melbourne Cricket Ground) fall toward the upper end of these ranges. The boundary line marks the perimeter of the playing surface; the ball is out of bounds when it crosses this line entirely.

At each end of the ground stands a set of four posts: two central goal posts and two outer behind posts, as described in Law 1.3. Goal posts must be a minimum of 6m in height above the playing surface, spaced exactly 6.4m apart. Behind posts, positioned on either side of the goal posts, must be a minimum of 3m in height and are also spaced 6.4m from their adjacent goal post, giving a total goal-to-behind span defined precisely under Law 1.3(a)–(c).

The centre of the ground features a centre square measuring 50m x 50m, within which centre bounces occur at the start of each quarter and after each goal, per Law 1.4 and Law 6. A centre circle of 3m diameter, bisected by a centre line, is marked at the middle of the centre square. Additional markings include 50-metre arcs extending from each goal, used to determine free kick advancement distances under Law 15 (Free Kicks) and kick-in procedures under Law 17.

Interchange (bench) areas are marked alongside the boundary line for player rotations, as regulated under Law 5 (Interchange), permitting teams to make interchanges at any time during play subject to competition-specific interchange cap rules.

Section 4: Players & Officials

Each team fields 18 players on the ground at any one time, as prescribed by Law 5.1, with additional interchange players (typically four, per current AFL competition rules) permitted to rotate on and off through the interchange zone during play. Traditional playing positions — full forward, centre half-forward, wing, centre, ruck, half-back, and full back, among others — are not mandated by the Laws themselves but are conventional team structures recognized throughout the sport.

Match officials include field umpires (typically three in AFL premiership matches), who are responsible for interpreting and enforcing the Laws of the Game, awarding free kicks, and bouncing or throwing the ball up to restart play, as outlined in Law 6 and Law 19 (Field Umpires). Boundary umpires (typically two) determine when the ball is out of bounds and perform the boundary throw-in under Law 18. Goal umpires (one per end) signal goals and behinds and confirm scoring outcomes under Law 21 (Goal Umpires), often supported by score review technology at the AFL senior level.

The team captain, designated per club regulations, exercises specific responsibilities including coin toss participation before the match (Law 7) and communication with umpires on rules matters. Interchange stewards and timekeepers, while not officiating the Laws directly, manage interchange compliance and match timing respectively under supporting competition regulations.

Section 5: Rules of Play

A match consists of four quarters, with playing time set by competition regulations (commonly 20 minutes plus time-on at AFL senior level), as referenced in Law 8 (Duration of Match). Play commences each quarter, and resumes after each goal, with a field umpire's bounce or ball-up at the centre of the ground, per Law 6.

Players may dispose of the ball by kicking, handballing (handpassing), or running while bouncing the ball at least once every 15 metres, in accordance with Law 15 (Running With the Ball). A legal handball (Law 15.2) requires the ball to be struck with a clenched fist while held in the other hand; throwing the ball is prohibited and penalized as a "throw" under Law 18.5.

Marking is a fundamental rule of Australian Football: a player who cleanly catches a kicked ball that has traveled at least 15 metres without being touched by another player or the ground is awarded a mark, entitling them to an unimpeded kick or handball from that spot, per Law 16 (The Mark). Tackling is permitted between the shoulders and knees; high contact, dangerous tackles, and tackles below the knees are prohibited and penalized under Law 18 (Reportable Offences and Free Kicks).

Free kicks are awarded for infringements including holding the ball (failure to dispose of the ball correctly when tackled, Law 18.4), holding the man (illegally impeding an opponent without the ball, Law 18.3), high contact, in the back, and other breaches enumerated in Law 18. When a free kick is awarded, the offending player must retreat the distance specified by the umpire (typically stepping back or standing on the mark), per Law 18.9.

Section 6: Scoring

Scoring is governed by Law 21 of the Laws of Australian Football. A goal, worth six points, is scored when the ball is propelled by a kick from the attacking team through the goal posts (the central two of the four posts at each end) without touching either post or being touched by any player, per Law 21.2.

A behind, worth one point, is scored when the ball passes between a goal post and an adjacent behind post, when the ball touches a goal post before passing through, or when the ball is forced through the goal by any means other than a clean kick from the attacking team (for example, touched en route, or forced through by an off-target kick or by a defender), as detailed in Law 21.3–21.5.

The team with the higher combined score (goals multiplied by six, plus behinds) at the conclusion of the fourth quarter is declared the winner, per Law 3.2. Should scores be level at full-time, the match result is recorded as a draw in AFL home-and-away premiership matches, while finals matches are subject to extra-time provisions specified in competition-specific bylaws referenced alongside Law 8.

Section 7: Violations & Penalties

Free kicks are the primary sanction for on-field infringements under Law 18, awarded to the non-offending team and taken from the point of the infringement (or, for certain offences, at the discretion of the field umpire in accordance with the "advantage" provisions of Law 18.10). Common free-kick offences include high contact, in the back, holding the ball, holding the man, tripping, chopping the arms, deliberate out of bounds, and dangerous tackles.

For more serious infringements, umpires may award a 50-metre penalty in addition to a free kick, advancing the mark toward the offending team's goal, as prescribed under Law 18.9 for offences such as dissent, deliberate delay, or a player entering the protected area around a mark or free kick.

Reportable offences — including strong or forceful contact to the head or neck, misconduct, and other serious infringements defined under Law 18.6 and the AFL's Match Review process — are referred to the Match Review Officer after the match for potential suspension or fine, independent of any free kick awarded during play. The severity of sanctions is assessed against a graded system considering impact, contact area, and conduct, as published in the AFL's annual Match Review guidelines that operate alongside the Laws of the Game.

Section 8: Safety Considerations

The Laws of Australian Football place significant emphasis on player safety, particularly regarding head and neck contact. Law 18.6 explicitly prohibits forceful contact to the head or neck of an opponent, reflecting the AFL's ongoing concussion-management focus. Players suspected of concussion are subject to mandatory removal from play under the AFL's Concussion and Head Trauma Management Guidelines, which operate in conjunction with the Laws of the Game and require independent medical clearance before a player may return to the field, both within the same match and in subsequent weeks.

Dangerous tackling — including tackles that drive an opponent into the ground with excessive force, tackles below the knees, and tackles that pin the arms while driving the head/neck into the turf — is specifically legislated against under Law 18, reflecting law changes in recent seasons aimed at reducing high-risk contact.

Padding on the base of goal and behind posts is mandated under Law 1 to reduce injury risk from high-speed collisions near the boundary and goal square. Grounds must also meet minimum surface and boundary clearance standards under competition regulations to protect players from hazards near the perimeter.

Umpires are empowered under Law 4.2 to instruct players to remove dangerous jewellery, unpadded equipment, or other hazardous items before or during play. Clubs and competitions additionally enforce mouthguard and protective equipment requirements at community and junior levels through affiliated state and territory football body regulations, supplementing the national Laws of the Game.
